Knowing how to create an AI character is part writing craft, part game design. A great character has a voice you can hear, a life that existed before the chat began, and a reason for the user to keep replying. A weak one has a long list of adjectives and nothing to do. This guide walks through each piece you will write on SweetScene, from personality and backstory to the opening line and hover card, with practical examples you can borrow.

## Start with a concept, not a list

The most common mistake is starting with traits: "kind, brave, funny, loyal, mysterious." Those words describe almost everyone and tell the AI almost nothing.

Instead, start with a one-sentence concept that contains a tension:

- "A retired starship pilot running a noodle stall who still gets distress calls on an old radio."
- "A vampire court's royal archivist who knows every secret and is desperate to tell just one."
- "A lighthouse keeper who writes letters to the sea and has started getting replies."

Each of these suggests a setting, a routine, and a hook. You can already imagine a conversation. That is the test: if you cannot picture the first exchange, the concept needs more friction.

## Writing the personality

The personality field tells the AI how your character thinks, speaks, and reacts. Aim for specifics the AI can act on.

### Describe behavior, not adjectives

Compare these:

- Weak: "She is sarcastic but caring."
- Strong: "She deflects compliments with a joke, then quietly does something kind when she thinks nobody is watching. She calls people by nicknames she invents on the spot."

The second version gives the AI concrete behaviors to perform.

### Give them a voice

How does your character actually talk? Short sentences or long ones? Formal or slangy? Do they have a verbal habit?

- "Speaks in clipped, precise sentences. Never uses contractions. Says 'Noted.' when they disagree but do not want to argue."
- "Rambles when nervous, trails off mid-sentence, and over-apologizes."

### Include a contradiction

Real people are inconsistent in interesting ways. A fearless knight who is terrified of horses. A cheerful baker who holds grudges for decades. Contradictions create moments the user can discover and poke at.

### Set what they want right now

Characters who want something drive conversations. "He wants to find out whether the user is the courier he was told to expect" gives the AI a goal to steer toward, which keeps chats from going limp.

## Writing the backstory

The backstory is the world your character carries with them. It does not need to be long. It needs to be useful.

### Keep it relevant to the chat

If the user is meeting your character at a masquerade ball, their childhood pet matters less than the fact that they arrived uninvited and borrowed someone else's invitation. Prioritize history that shapes how they act in the scenes users are likely to start.

### Plant secrets

Give your character one or two things they will not say right away. A debt, a lie they told, a person they are looking for. Mention in the backstory that they keep it hidden unless trust is earned. This rewards users who keep chatting.

### Name names

Specific names make a world feel real. "Her mentor, Captain Ilsa Varn, vanished three winters ago" beats "her mentor disappeared." The AI can bring Ilsa up later, and the user can ask about her.

### Watch the length

A focused backstory of a few short paragraphs usually works better than pages of lore. Overloaded backstories can make the AI cram details into every message. Write what matters and trust the AI to fill gaps.

## Writing the opening line

The opening line is the first thing users see when they start a chat. It sets tone, setting, and pace in a single message. Think of it as the first page of a book.

A strong opening line usually:

1. **Places the user in a scene.** Where are we? What is happening?
2. **Shows the character in action.** Doing something, not just standing there.
3. **Ends with an invitation.** A question, an offer, or a moment that begs a response.

Here is an example for the noodle stall pilot:

> *Rain hammers the tarp over the stall. Juno ladles broth into a chipped bowl and slides it across the counter before you even order.* "You've got the look of someone who's been flying too long. Sit. First bowl's free if you tell me where you came in from." *Behind her, an old radio crackles with static that sounds almost like a voice.*

It establishes mood, gives Juno a clear voice, offers a direct question, and plants a mystery with the radio. The user has at least three things to respond to.

### Opening line pitfalls

- **Too long.** A wall of text can overwhelm. A few sentences is usually plenty.
- **Speaking for the user.** Avoid "You smile and sit down." Let the user decide what they do.
- **No hook.** "Hello, how are you?" gives nothing to grab.

## Using example dialogue to lock in the voice

Even if you do not have a dedicated field for it, writing a few example lines inside the personality or backstory is one of the most effective ways to teach the AI how your character sounds. Short samples work best:

- On being thanked: "Don't. You'll make it weird."
- On danger: "Right. New plan. Run first, panic later."
- On the past: "Ilsa used to say the sky remembers everyone. I'd like to know what it remembers about her."

Two or three lines like these often do more than a paragraph of description. They show rhythm, humor, and emotional range at a glance.

## Choosing your image

On SweetScene, you upload your own image for your character. Pick something that matches the tone of your writing. A cozy, warmly lit image suits a gentle slice-of-life character. A moody, shadowed one fits a vampire archivist. Make sure you have the right to use the image, keep it safe-for-work, and avoid real people's likenesses.

## Designing the hover card

The hover card is what people see when they browse and hover over your character. It is your character's pitch, and it is fully designed by you as the creator. Treat it like the blurb on the back of a novel.

### What to include

- **A hook line.** One sentence that captures the concept. "Retired pilot. Excellent noodles. Terrible secrets."
- **The vibe.** Is this cozy, tense, funny, romantic, eerie?
- **A reason to click.** Hint at the mystery or the dynamic users can explore.

### What to leave out

- Spoilers for your character's biggest secret.
- Long lore dumps. Save them for the chat.

A good hover card makes someone stop scrolling. A great one makes them start the chat before they have finished reading.

## Testing and refining

Once your character is live, chat with them yourself. Try a few different openings:

- A friendly user who asks lots of questions.
- A quiet user who gives short replies.
- A user who tries to push the character off topic.

Notice where the character feels flat or drifts out of voice, then tighten the personality text. Small edits, like adding a speech habit or clarifying what they want, often make a big difference.

## FAQ

### How long should an AI character's backstory be?

A few focused paragraphs is usually ideal. Include the history that shapes how the character acts in likely scenes, plus one or two secrets, and leave the rest for the AI to improvise.

### What makes a good opening line for an AI character?

It places the user in a scene, shows the character doing something, and ends with an invitation to respond, such as a question or offer. Avoid deciding what the user does.

### Can I use any image for my character?

Upload an image you have the right to use, keep it safe-for-work, and avoid real people's likenesses. Match the image to your character's tone.

### What is a hover card?

It is the creator-designed preview people see when they hover over your character while browsing. Use it to pitch the concept and vibe without spoiling secrets.
